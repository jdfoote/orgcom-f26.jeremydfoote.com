Case Study: The Lodestar Launch Decision

Background

Lodestar Electric is a mid-sized electric vehicle manufacturer preparing to launch its first mass-market model, the Lodestar S. The launch has been in development for three years and is scheduled for eight weeks from now. Two thousand dealerships have pre-sold 18,000 units. The CEO has already appeared on the cover of Forbes with the headline “America’s EV Moment.”

One week before the final go/no-go decision meeting, the lead battery engineer, Daria Chen, submits an internal report flagging a thermal management issue. Under sustained high-speed driving in temperatures above 95°F, the battery cooling system may underperform, causing reduced range and, in rare cases, a risk of thermal runaway (battery fire). Her report estimates the probability of thermal runaway at approximately 1 in 4,000 vehicles under worst-case conditions.

What Happened in the Meeting

The go/no-go meeting includes the CEO, CFO, VP of Engineering, VP of Marketing, General Counsel, and Daria Chen. Here is a summary of what each person said:

  • CEO: “We’ve spent $340 million getting here. Our competitors are six months behind us. Missing this launch window costs us everything. Daria, how confident are you in that 1-in-4,000 figure?”
  • CFO: “A recall post-launch would be catastrophic financially. But delaying also has massive costs. I’d want to see the data independently verified before we decide.”
  • VP of Engineering: “Every vehicle has edge-case risks. We exceeded all current federal safety standards. I think we’re fine.”
  • VP of Marketing: “If we delay now, the press narrative becomes ‘Lodestar can’t deliver.’ We can issue a software update post-launch to manage the thermal curve.”
  • General Counsel: “Federal standards are met. As long as we document that we reviewed the report, our liability exposure is limited.”
  • Daria Chen: “I want to be clear: I’m not saying don’t launch. I’m saying we need four more weeks to redesign the cooling loop. The fix is straightforward. The risk is low but it’s real.”

The CEO called for a show of hands. Everyone except Daria voted to launch on schedule.

Group Tasks

Each group will analyze the case through one assigned decision-making model, then share findings with the class.

Group 1: The Rational Model

The rational model assumes decision-makers have complete information, evaluate all alternatives, and choose the option that maximizes outcomes.

  • In what ways did the Lodestar meeting follow the rational model?
  • In what ways did it fail to follow it? What information was missing, ignored, or not sought?
  • If the team had followed a fully rational process, what would the decision-making procedure have looked like — and would it have changed the outcome?

Group 2: Bounded Rationality

Herbert Simon argued that real decision-makers satisfice — they look for a “good enough” solution rather than the optimal one, because their information and cognitive capacity are limited.

  • What cognitive shortcuts or “satisficing” moves do you see in the meeting? Who was settling for “good enough,” and on what basis?
  • What information did participants use as a mental shortcut to avoid deeper analysis?
  • How did time pressure and sunk costs (“we’ve spent $340 million”) shape the decision? Is this rational, boundedly rational, or something else?

Group 3: Groupthink

Irving Janis identified groupthink as a pattern where the pressure to reach consensus overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives. Classic symptoms include: illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalization, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, and pressure on dissenters.

  • Which groupthink symptoms appear in this meeting? Find specific evidence in the dialogue.
  • What communication dynamics silenced or minimized Daria Chen’s concern?
  • What specific procedural change — in how the meeting was structured or how voices were solicited — might have produced a different outcome?

Group 4: The Garbage Can Model

The garbage can model describes organizations as “organized anarchies” where problems, solutions, participants, and choice opportunities flow independently and connect somewhat randomly.

  • What different “problems” and “solutions” were floating around in this meeting, and did they actually match each other?
  • Is the go/no-go decision actually about the thermal issue — or are other problems (financial pressure, competitive timing, reputation) being channeled into this decision?
  • Who were the key participants, and how did their presence or absence shape what got decided?

Whole-Class Discussion

After groups share out, we’ll discuss together:

  • Which model best explains what happened at Lodestar?
  • Are these models competing explanations, or can they all be true at the same time?
  • What does this case reveal about the role of communication — not just information — in organizational decision-making?
