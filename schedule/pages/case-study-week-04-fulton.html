Your Organization

Fulton Street Academy is a K–8 charter school on Indianapolis’s west side with about 520 students, 95% of whom qualify for free or reduced lunch. The school operates under what’s sometimes called a “no excuses” model: high behavioral expectations (uniforms, silent hallway walking, no calling out), extended school hours (7:30 AM to 4:30 PM), and a relentless focus on academic achievement as measured by standardized tests. The school’s test scores are among the highest in the district.

The school has 35 teachers and 10 instructional support staff, plus an administrative team of 5. Teacher turnover is high — about 25% per year. Most teachers are young (median age 27), many are recent college graduates who entered through a competitive teaching fellowship. The principal has been in place for four years and holds a very clear vision for the school’s culture and instruction.

Teachers have very little autonomy over curriculum — it is scripted and standardized across grade levels. A math teacher and a reading teacher on the same grade level teach the same lesson on the same day using the same materials. Instructional coaches observe classrooms weekly and give feedback against a detailed rubric. Teachers who receive low marks on the rubric get a “growth plan” with specific targets.

The school is explicit that it holds teachers to the same high expectations it holds students. Internal communications from the principal tend to be warm and motivational in tone (“We are a family working toward something that matters”) while simultaneously being very directive about practice. The school has a weekly all-staff meeting on Fridays, but it is used mainly for announcements and celebrating student wins — not for discussion or decision-making.

Some teachers love it: they describe the clear structure, the sense of mission, and the measurable results as reasons they stay. Others burn out quickly, saying they feel like they have no professional judgment and no real voice. A veteran teacher who left last year said: “I wasn’t a teacher, I was a script-reader. They hired smart people and then refused to use their minds.”
