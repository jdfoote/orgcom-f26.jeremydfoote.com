Big Picture: What Are We Even Doing?

  • All six approaches are trying to solve the same problem: how do organizations work?
  • Each one is a reaction to the limits of the one before it
  • Know what each approach says about: the worker, communication, and power

Ch. 1: Communication & the Modern World

Models of communication:

  • S-M-C-R / transmission model — linear, sender → receiver; what are its limits?
  • Constitutive model — communication creates shared meaning, not just transmits it
  • Rhetorical model — communication as a “practical art of discourse”

Ch. 1: Weick & Complexity

  • Requisite variety: organizations must be as complicated as the problems they face — a simple org can’t handle a complex environment
  • Equivocality: ambiguity in the environment; organizations develop rules and cycles to reduce it

Ch. 1: Forces of Change

  • Globalization: outsourcing, exploitation risks, domestic job loss, interdependence of economies
  • Demographics: generational cohorts, “majority-minority” shift in the U.S., diversity in the workforce
  • Technology: virtual organizations (no physical “brick and mortar” presence)
  • Climate/environment, homeland security

Ch. 2: The Classical Approach

Three theorists — know who said what:

Theorist Key Idea
Taylor Scientific Management; time-motion studies; one best way
Fayol 14 Principles; prescriptive; span of control; unity of command
Weber Bureaucracy; three types of authority

Ch. 2: Taylor’s Scientific Management

  • Time and motion studies: find the single most efficient way to do a task
  • Workers tend toward soldiering (working slower than possible) — management must control pace
  • Separate planning (managers) from doing (workers)
  • Incentive pay for efficiency

Ch. 2: Fayol’s Principles

  • Prescriptive — tells managers what should happen
  • Unity of command: employees receive orders from one person only
  • Division of labor: employees do a limited, specialized set of tasks
  • Span of control: first-level managers supervise 20–30 employees
  • Remuneration: year-end bonuses and incentives

Ch. 2: Weber’s Authority

Three types — know the difference:

  • Traditional authority — power from custom or family lineage (e.g., family business)
  • Charismatic authority — power from personal qualities or reputation
  • Rational-legal (bureaucratic) authority — power from rules and position — this is what Weber advocated

Ch. 2: The Machine Metaphor

  • Workers are standardized, interchangeable parts
  • Organizations are predictable because machines follow rules
  • Communication flows vertically/downward, formal, written (codified)
  • Horizontal communication is discouraged

Ch. 3: The Human Relations Approach

Hawthorne Studies (Western Electric, 1920s–30s):

  • Started with a classical question: does lighting affect productivity?
  • Relay Assembly Room: productivity went up regardless of conditions → social factors matter
  • Bank Wiring Room: workers held each other to a group norm — social group overrode management
  • Interview Program: workers wanted to talk about feelings, not just work

Ch. 3: Hawthorne Effect

  • People change their behavior when they know they’re being observed
  • The finding that changed everything: social relationships shape worker behavior more than physical conditions or pay

Ch. 3: Maslow’s Hierarchy

  • Five levels: physiological → safety → social (belonging) → esteem → self-actualization
  • Lower needs must be satisfied before higher needs become motivating
  • Safety needs example: protective equipment, job security

Ch. 3: Theory X and Theory Y (McGregor)

Theory X Theory Y
Workers are lazy, avoid work Workers want to work if conditions are right
Must be controlled and threatened Workers will self-direct if committed
Communication is top-down, controlling Communication is participatory
  • Sager’s research found Theory X managers used more controlling communication patterns

Ch. 3: Managerial Grid & Likert

  • Blake & Mouton’s Managerial Grid: two axes — concern for people and concern for production
    • Ideal: Team Management (high-high)
    • Country Club: high people, low production
  • Likert’s System IV: control exercised at all levels, not just the top — participative management

Ch. 3: Human Relations vs. Human Resources

  • Human Relations: treat workers better to get more out of them — surface-level changes
  • Human Resources: genuinely involve workers’ cognitive contributions — ideas, judgment, decisions
  • Human relations can be misused as manipulation to get compliance without real change

Ch. 3: Other HR Concepts

  • Knowledge management: the cycle of capturing, sharing, and applying employee knowledge
  • Learning organization (Senge): emphasizes mental flexibility and team learning
  • TQM (Total Quality Management): high failure rate — why?

Ch. 4: Systems Theory

  • Emerged from biology (not engineering or management)
  • Core shift: organizations are open systems interacting with their environment

Key properties:

  • Holism: the whole is more than the sum of its parts
  • Interdependence: components rely on each other
  • Hierarchical ordering: systems are nested within larger systems
  • Permeability: boundaries are open (not fully closed)

Ch. 4: ITO + Feedback

  • Input → Throughput → Output model
  • Positive (amplifying) feedback: more leads to even more (growth, escalation)
  • Negative (stabilizing) feedback: deviation triggers correction back toward baseline
  • Equifinality: multiple paths can lead to the same outcome

Ch. 4: Network Concepts

  • Network density: how many connections exist relative to the possible connections
  • Network symmetry: whether links flow equally in both directions
  • Bridge: connects two otherwise separate groups
  • Liaison: connects groups without belonging to either
  • Isolate: rarely communicates with others

Ch. 4: Weick’s Model of Organizing

  • Goal of organizations: reduce equivocality (make sense of an ambiguous environment)
  • Enactment → Selection → Retention: how organizations make sense of inputs
  • Assembly rules: saved guidelines for handling situations you’ve encountered before
  • Environment is something organizations enact, not something that just exists out there

Ch. 4: Organizational Culture

Prescriptive approach (Deal & Kennedy; Peters & Waterman):

  • Strong cultures → better performance
  • Deal & Kennedy: 4 components of strong culture (values, heroes, rites/rituals, cultural network)
  • Peters & Waterman: 8 themes of “excellent” organizations

Alternative approaches: culture is not unitary; multiple subcultures; ambiguity is normal

Ch. 4: Schein’s Three Levels

  • Artifacts: visible symbols, practices, physical space
  • Espoused values: stated beliefs and goals
  • Basic assumptions: deep, taken-for-granted beliefs (hardest to see, most powerful)
  • Cultural performances are interactional, contextual, episodic, and improvisational — not separate from history

Ch. 6: The Critical Approach

  • Philosophical roots: Karl Marx and the Frankfurt School
  • Key claim: organizations are sites of domination, not neutral coordination
  • Goal of critical theory: emancipation — freeing people from oppressive structures
  • Role of the critical theorist is like a therapist: help people see what they can’t see themselves

Ch. 6: Power Concepts

  • Ideology: taken-for-granted beliefs that make the status quo seem natural and inevitable
  • Hegemony: the controlled group doesn’t just accept control — they actively reproduce it
  • Alienation (Marx): workers become separated from the product of their labor

Two views of power:

  • Traditional: power held by individuals, measurable
  • Communicative/relational: power emerges from communication and relationships

Ch. 6: Three Types of Control

Type How it works
Simple Direct, authoritarian — boss tells you what to do
Technical Built into the tools and processes (machine pace, monitoring)
Concertive Peers enforce norms — the group polices itself

Ch. 6: Resistance

  • Resistance = workers opposing and counteracting authority
  • Overt: unions, strikes, walkouts
  • Covert: working slowly, ignoring rules, sharing restricted information
  • Robin Clair’s work: frames are used to normalize and justify harassment — e.g., “that’s just how it is here”

Ch. 6: Feminist Approaches

  • Core argument: organizations are built around gendered assumptions — they’re not neutral
  • Ideal worker norm: the model worker has no care responsibilities outside work
  • Glass ceiling: barriers to advancement that are real but officially denied
  • Intersectionality: gender, race, class, disability combine — different people experience the same org differently

Ch. 6: Streams of Feminist Thought

  • Liberal feminism: work within the system for equal opportunity
  • Radical feminism: the system itself is the problem; need fundamental transformation
  • Standpoint feminism: knowledge comes from marginalized positions; remedies come from outside the system
  • Postmodern feminism: amplify marginalized voices in societal dialogue

Things That Are Commonly Confused

  • Human Relations vs. Human Resources — one is surface, one involves real participation
  • Positive vs. Negative feedback loops — “positive” doesn’t mean good; it means amplifying
  • Holism — the system is more than the sum of parts (not less)
  • Hegemony vs. simple control — hegemony means the dominated participate in their own control
  • Theory X vs. Y — these are manager assumptions, not worker types
